A little while ago I made the rounds in Silicon Valley, and noticed something: a lot of companies have quietly started running the open model from a company called Kimi.

One friend took it further. He uses AI to help design AI chips — and those chips, in turn, run the AI. A closed loop. I asked him why he wasn't just using one of the big closed-source names. His answer: the customer demands that all data stay on-site — not a single byte leaves the building. So the model has to move into their own machine room. And of the models you can actually move in-house and still enjoy using, Kimi kept coming out on top.

Fair enough. So let's see what it's made of. With Claude's help I pulled apart Kimi K3's architecture and weights. You don't need to know any of the math — I'll keep it in plain language, and walk through the three most fun bits.

First, the basics worth holding onto: 2.8 trillion parameters, 93 layers, a one-million-token context window, and it reads images natively. The team says that thanks to the design choices below, it squeezes roughly 2.5× more capability out of the same compute than the previous generation, K2.

01

It stops re-reading the whole book. It takes notes.Kimi Delta Attention — KDA

What a language model does all day is humble: read some text, guess the next word. But to guess well, it first has to understand each word — and a word's meaning depends on the words around it.

So how does a traditional model "understand"? Every time it reads a new word, it flips back through every word it has read so far. Reach the millionth word, and it re-reads the previous nine-hundred-thousand-plus, just to make sense of that one. Exhausting, right? That's exactly why old models crawl and stutter the moment the text gets long.

Kimi K3 says: why re-read anything — just keep a notebook. It maintains a fixed-size little notebook (the term is Kimi Delta Attention, KDA); each new word only updates the notebook, never sends it flipping back. The notebook stays the same size no matter how thick the book gets, so it never overflows.

There's a nice refinement, too: different columns fade at different speeds — keep the important stuff for many pages, let the trivia slip away at once. Same as your own bookkeeping: you remember the rent precisely, and you've long forgotten the two dollars you spent on water yesterday.

The official framing KDA takes Gated DeltaNet and adds "finer-grained gating," so a finite memory gets spent more wisely. Paired with full attention, it trims the KV cache by about 75% and speeds up long-context decoding by up to 6×.

Notice that S — its shape is fixed ([B, HV, K, V]), and has nothing to do with how long your text is. That's the root reason it can read a million words without blowing up: the notebook's size is constant, only its contents keep changing.

And "different columns fade at different speeds" lives inside that g. Its real formula in the layer is g = -exp(A_log) · softplus(...) — one decay speed per dimension, with the minus sign and softplus there to pin it firmly into a sane range so it doesn't explode in low precision. Fancy-sounding; in plain terms, it's a "how-fast-to-forget" dial for each column.

A bonus: it drops a whole step — no seat numbers

Traditional models stamp a number on every word (that's RoPE) to say who comes before whom. K3 simply doesn't (that's NoPE) — because the notebook is written in order by nature: whatever was written first sits earlier, so the ordering is baked into the act of writing. Skipping that step also shakes off an old headache: no more "we ran out of seat numbers, time to re-number everything" when the text gets very long.

This is where K3 parts ways with Llama and DeepSeek at the deepest level: those two still build on traditional full/soft attention, while K3 is the largest public model to date that dares to make linear attention the main act.

02

It refuses to play the telephone game.Attention Residuals

You've played telephone, right? A line of people, each whispering to the next, and "I ate a whole watermelon" arrives at the end as "I hate the old water balloon."

Inside a big model, the same whispering happens — it's stacked in layers, and information passes upward from the bottom. How many layers does Kimi K3 stack? 93 (the last generation had just 61). Ninety-three people passing a message down the line; by the top, the original sentence is long gone.

The old fix was a patch: besides hearing the previous layer, each layer could also glance at the original. It helps — but 93 layers is deep, and it's still blurry.

K3's move is clever: it lets a later layer turn around and ask any earlier layer directly — "wait, what did you actually say?" — and decide for itself: trust this layer 60%, that one 10%. No more depending on a version that's been whispered ninety times over. Want someone's words? Go straight to the source.

The official framing They call it Attention Residuals. In one line: let each layer selectively pull from earlier layers' representations, instead of piling them up uniformly. The cost is negligible (one small extra vector per layer); the payoff is real — the team reports ~25% better training efficiency for under 2% added cost.

This is what lets K3 stack all the way to 93 layers and keep getting smarter instead of muddier.

03

896 consultants on the books — and it barely spends a dime.Stable LatentMoE

Inside Kimi K3 sits a small army of "expert consultants" — 896 of them. Sounds like a payroll headed for bankruptcy, except it isn't, because of one house rule: for any given question, only the 16 most relevant experts are called into the meeting (the term is Stable LatentMoE — 896 routed experts, 16 activated, plus 2 always-on ones). A thousand names on the roster, but only ~1.8% used each time: affordable to keep, and cheap to convene.

Anyone who's managed a team knows there are two traps here. K3 has an answer for each.

Trap one: all the work goes to a few stars, everyone else slacks off

Managers love handing work to the people they trust — so the star experts get worked to death while eight hundred others sip tea. K3 brings in a stone-faced dispatcher (Quantile Balancing) who ignores reputation and looks at one thing only: how much have you already taken on today? The busy ones step back, the idle ones step up — forced fair shares all round.

Trap two: to assign work, you'd photocopy the thick file 896 times

For all 896 to be assignable, the naïve way copies the whole task packet to everyone — death by photocopier. K3's fix: compress the thick file into a one-page summary first, then hand it out (project each token into a half-width latent space before dispatching). Saves paper, saves time, saves the office from a room full of smoking printers.

Two more small touches

Born able to see. Many models only read text; to show them an image you bolt on a pair of "eyes," awkwardly. K3 comes with eyes built in (its vision module, MoonViT-V2, trained from scratch). Throw it a screenshot of a webpage and it can write the front-end code to match — which is a big part of why it's so strong at turning pictures into code.

Bookkeeping in a pocket notebook. For the same ledger, some use a thick account book; others keep it just as clearly in a palm-sized one. From training onward, K3 forced itself to keep the books in "pocket" form (the low-precision MXFP4 format) — and still keeps them accurately. So even carrying 2.8 trillion parameters, it takes up less room than you'd expect and doesn't burn as much to run.

Back to that "closed loop"

By now you can probably see why my friend chose it. If the data has to stay on-site, the model has to move in-house — and moving in-house, you dread two things above all: too slow, too expensive. Every trick above — take notes, don't play telephone, keep consultants without overspending, book-keep in a pocket notebook — is hammering away at exactly those two words: fast and cheap. Add that it's open source, weights in hand to modify at will, and the loop starts turning: AI designs a better chip, and the better chip runs that same AI faster and cheaper.

Studying it this far, I felt something. Take any one of these ideas alone and you'd shrug — "that's it? not even complicated." But it's precisely a pile of "not even complicated" cleverness, stacked together, that turns a giant into something fast, cheap, and small enough to move home. The genuinely impressive things rarely come from one earth-shaking masterstroke — they come from a heap of small, well-placed cleverness.

Two buckets of cold water, as usual

It's useful and cheap — but the very top closed-source models are still a notch stronger overall, something Kimi itself admits.

It can move in-house, but "in-house" here means machine-room scale — not something your gaming PC can hold.

And its open-source license is not a free-for-all: read the terms carefully before you build a real business on it.

As for how far this road runs — pull up a chair and keep watching.

A2

A pocket glossary

The metaphors above are enough — but in case you want to sound sharp at the next dinner table, here are the terms:

KV cachekey/value cache
Where a model stores the "ledger" of words it has read, so it doesn't recompute. The old way swells as text grows, eating memory. KDA swaps it for a fixed-size state — hence ~75% saved.
Linear attentionlinear attention
A family of methods whose compute grows linearly with length instead of exploding quadratically. KDA is one of them.
KDAKimi Delta Attention
Kimi's in-house linear attention. Descends from DeltaNet and Gated DeltaNet; K3's twist upgrades "one blanket forget switch" into "a forget dial per dimension."
Delta ruledelta rule
When updating the notebook, write only the new part rather than rewriting the whole book. Step (2) in the code above.
MLAMulti-head Latent Attention
DeepSeek's KV-saving global attention. K3 keeps one layer in four as MLA, to handle the precise long-range look-ups that linear attention is weaker at.
RoPE / NoPEposition encoding
RoPE is the mainstream way of "stamping seat numbers" on words; NoPE means not stamping at all — K3 leans on KDA's built-in ordering and drops the seat numbers.
MoEMixture of Experts
Split one big model into many "experts," call only a small handful each time — so it holds many parameters but computes only a little per step.
Stable LatentMoEStable LatentMoE
K3's MoE scheme: 896 routed experts + 2 always-on, 16 active per token. "Latent" = compress the material to half width before handing it out, to cut communication.
Quantile BalancingQuantile Balancing
The dispatcher that "forces fair shares by workload." The upside: no need for the old trick of adding a fussy extra loss term.
Attention ResidualsAttention Residuals
The mechanism that lets "later layers turn around and name-ask earlier layers." Cures the blur of a 93-layer stack.
MXFP4quantization-aware training
A "pocket notebook" low-precision format. K3 trains in it from the start, so 2.8T parameters take up less room than you'd think.
MoonViT-V2vision module
K3's eyes. The notable part: trained from scratch (skipping the industry-standard contrastive warm-up) yet still reads images sharply.
TPOTtime per output token
How long you wait for each word out. KDA makes it up to ~6× faster on long text — which feels like "the typing stopped lagging."
